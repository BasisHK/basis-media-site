The 9-second rule for Hong Kong short-form

Between Central and Admiralty you get about nine seconds of a stranger's life. We broke down retention curves from 300+ HK videos to find where people actually decide to stay.

5 min readBasis Media HK

Hong Kong attention isn’t abstract. It’s geographic. A stop on the Island Line. The pause between doors closing and the next announcement. If your hook can’t land in that window, the rest of the edit is a polite theory.

We pulled retention curves across more than 300 Hong Kong short-form pieces — our own work and public posts from F&B, retail, and local services — and looked for the beat where viewers decide to stay. The pattern kept repeating around the nine-second mark.

Nine seconds isn’t a format length. It’s a verdict. After that, you’re either a story or a skip.

What happens before second nine

Most drop-off is decided earlier — first 1–2 seconds for pattern interrupt, then a second cliff when people realise the video isn’t about them. But the survivors of those cliffs still face a quieter decision around second nine: is there a reason to keep watching, or was that just a clever open?

Clips that held past nine seconds usually did one of three things by then:

  • Showed a payoff already in progress (food hitting the plate, a queue moving, a product in use)
  • Introduced a person with a stake — owner, creator, customer — not a brand voiceover
  • Asked or implied a question only the next few seconds could answer

Retention note

Logo-first opens underperformed across categories. Place-first opens (a street, a stall, a familiar corner) held better with local audiences — recognition is a hook.

Design for the commute, not the couch

Global short-form advice assumes lean-back viewing. Hong Kong viewing is often standing, one-handed, sound optional, interrupted by a station name. That means text has to work. Faces help. Slow cinematic establishes do not.

When we storyboard now, we mark a hard checkpoint at 0:09: if the viewer still doesn’t know what this is or why it matters, we cut earlier material — we don’t add more explanation.

A practical rule for briefs

Write the first nine seconds as a standalone tip. If it can’t be forwarded without the rest of the video, it isn’t a hook yet — it’s an intro. Intros belong in brand films. Tips belong in feeds.

Central to Admiralty is short. Your open should be shorter than the ride feels.
